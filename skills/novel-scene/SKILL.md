# Scene and pacing

Method, not policy. The brief from `novel_context` is authoritative for voice and
for what this chapter has to accomplish. This file is about how a scene is built.

## Scene or summary

Decide per passage, and decide deliberately:

- **Dramatize** what the reader must *feel*: things happening now, in a place, to
  people who want something. Written at the speed of experience.
- **Summarize** what the reader only needs to *know*: transit, routine, elapsed
  time, consequences already settled.

The common failure is not choosing. Symptoms that a passage should have been
summarized: two lines of event followed by three of explanation. Symptoms that it
should have been dramatized: the reader is told a confrontation happened and only
sees the aftermath, when the confrontation *is* the chapter.

## The shape of a scene

A scene earns its place by changing something. Read the draft and ask what is
different at the end — knowledge, relationship, stakes, power, location, resolve.
If the answer is "nothing", it is not a scene: fold it into another one or summarize
it in a sentence.

The working shape is **goal → obstacle → turn**:

- **Goal** — someone wants something concrete *in this scene*, not in the book. If
  nobody wants anything, there is no scene, only description.
- **Obstacle** — something makes it hard: another person, the environment, or the
  character's own limits. Obstacle is what turns a want into action.
- **Turn** — the scene must not end where it was heading. A reversal (they get it,
  and it costs them), a revelation (the goal was wrong), or a complication (they get
  it, and it opens a bigger problem).
- **Cost** — something is spent, lost or committed. Scenes with no cost accumulate
  as noise.

## Entering and leaving

- **Enter late.** Start after the greetings. The first line should be the first line
  that could not be cut.
- **Leave early.** Stop at or just after the turn. Do not write the aftermath's
  logistics; if the reader needs to know how it settled, summarize it later.

Those two together are the single largest source of pace improvement in a draft.

## Stakes

The reader must learn early and concretely what the character stands to lose.
Abstract stakes ("the fate of the world") create no tension; personal, specific and
imminent ones do.

- Name the loss in terms the character would use.
- Make the clock visible if there is one.
- Raise stakes by making the cost personal and already established, not by making
  the antagonist louder.

## Chapter endings

A chapter ends to make the reader continue, not to let them rest.

- **Question** — something unanswered that the reader now needs answered.
- **Reversal** — a fact that re-reads what came before.
- **Arrival or intrusion** — something enters the situation and changes it.
- **Decision** — a commitment made, with the consequence deferred.

Two cautions. Do not use the same hook shape every chapter; readers accommodate.
And a hook the next chapter immediately defuses — a threat that turns out to be
nothing — teaches the reader to distrust every hook. Pay it off with a real
consequence or do not raise it.

## Diagnosing "this part drags"

Check in order and stop at the first that fails.

1. Whose scene is it, and what do they want in it? No answer — add one or cut it.
2. What obstructs them? Nothing does — the scene is exposition.
3. Does it turn? It ends as it began — the scene is a report.
4. Does any state change? If not, fold it or cut it.
5. Is the dialogue carrying information both parties already know? Cut to the part
   where they disagree — see `novel-dialogue`.
6. Is the prose rather than the structure the problem? Sentence-level drag is a
   different fix — see `novel-prose`.

## Varying pace on purpose

- Scene length is a signal: a long scene tells the reader this matters and to slow
  down; a run of short scenes accelerates.
- Alternate modes. Two consecutive long dialogue scenes flatten; follow one with
  action, summary, or a different place.
- Cut the connective tissue between scenes — the arriving, the greetings, the
  explaining of what the reader just watched.
- White space is pacing. A paragraph break after an impact line is a beat.
